## The short version

- **Civil war stays near its post-1946 high.** About one country in five (18%) had an active internal armed conflict in 2025. The central path rises to about 21% by the mid-2030s and is about 22% in 2065, with a 90% band from 11% to 40%; in about two worlds in three (67%) the 1990 record (23%) is broken at least once. (Until 29 October 2026 the 2065 path was about 18%: a code review found that every war starting after 2025 was treated as having a single rebel group for its whole life, which made it end too easily; rebel groups now multiply as wars age, as they did in 1950–2022.) (Until October 2026 the path eased to about 15%: peacekeeping was counted twice, once in the war-ending rate the simulator is aligned to and again on top of it, so wars ended too fast.)
- **Where the next crises are most likely, 2026–2030.** New civil war: Chad (66%), Libya (58%), Uganda (55%). Coups: Niger (38%), Guinea (37%), Madagascar (36%). Autocratization: Peru (64%), Georgia (51%), Bolivia (50%). Economic collapse: Congo and Palestine (38%), Equatorial Guinea (36%).
- **The headline includes AI, with its timing uncertain.** Each simulated world draws when intellectual work becomes nearly free, from an even mix of the Metaculus forecast, the AI Impacts 2023 survey of researchers (given that nothing has arrived by late 2026) and the Forecasting Research Institute's 2026 expert panel: by 2027 in 5% of worlds, by 2030 in 18%, by 2040 in 45% and by 2065 in 73%. General-purpose robots get their own, correlated arrival year (14% by 2030, 54% by 2040, 83% by 2065, counting robots that follow AI). Median world GDP per person in 2065 is $36k, against $25k if nothing changes, with a 90% band from $18k to $92k. The timing of AI and robots explains about a third of that spread (other breakthroughs, which AI speeds up, overlap with it), how large the AI growth dividend is another 5%, and the climate assumptions 5%. The gains go mostly to the frontier: with arrival in 2030 the United States ends 3.5 times richer than without AI, China 2.8 times, India 35% richer and Nigeria 2% richer. These are well below earlier versions because ageing now counts: a shrinking share of people of working age drags growth almost one for one (estimated on 1960–2023), costing China about 1 point of growth a year and Korea about 1.6, and the AI dividend now fades as ideas get harder to find.
- **Great-power war is a real tail risk, and war size matters more than war counts.** About 49% of worlds see a war of at least 1,000 battle deaths a year between great powers by 2065 (43% with no AI shock, 62% with 1914-style alliances). That now counts India: a great power is any of the traditional majors or a nuclear-armed state with at least 5% of world output. Among the traditional majors alone the figure is 26% (23% without AI, 42% with 1914-style alliances). Allies without a treaty now count: in about half the worlds where China and Taiwan go to war, the United States joins, so a China–US war rises to 11%. Most simulated fighting is small: a Russia–NATO clash happens in 34% of worlds but reaches war level in 15%; China–India clashes in 76% of worlds, fights a war in 27% and a war killing more than 100,000 in 13% (a war between two great powers draws its size from such wars in history). Total interstate battle deaths 2026–2065 have a median of 0.9 million (90% band 0.3–4.5 million; wars now last longer, as they have since 2010), and a single war passes 1 million battle deaths in 16% of worlds. A nuclear weapon is used in a war in about 14% of worlds (mostly a single use; a large exchange between great powers in 0.2%), and a new state gets the bomb in 71% (Iran in 53%), all on assumed rates.
- **2026 is already a war year.** Fighting recorded to September 2026 includes a war between Iran and Israel and the United States, and these count as fact, not forecast. Oil averaged 25% more than in 2025 in real terms, with peaks above $100. Without the Gulf war the model's oil index for 2026 would have been 97 (2025 = 100); with it, the model alone says 117, against 125 observed. The war costs Iran about 15% and Israel about 13% of output in 2027 and oil importers 0.3–0.5%; world output is 0.3% lower in 2027 and 0.7% lower by 2035.
- **More people, the same land.** Harvests grow with yields and with the land each country has left, demand grows with population and income. Cereal import dependency roughly doubles or more across the Sahel and East Africa by 2065 (Niger 23% to 64%, Chad and Mali from about 10% to 56%, Ethiopia 7% to 34%, Egypt 41% to 56%). The world grain price ends near today's level (median 87, 2025 = 100), within a wide band.
- **Democracy keeps eroding.** The world democracy index ends near 0.40 in 2065 (0.42 with no AI shock; it was 0.45 before free expression got its own equation on 30 October 2026, see below). After the US index fell from 0.84 to 0.74 in 2025, the five-year model gives a 28% chance of another fall that large by 2030.
- **Scored on the future, against crowds, and reviewed.** Trained only on data to 2024, the model ranked the only new civil war of 2025, South Sudan, 3rd of 142 countries, and a simple monthly version beat ViEWS, the leading academic system, on its own target. Against crowd forecasters the crowd wins clearly, on 63 questions about the use of force between states (Brier 0.100 against 0.132) and on 64 about leaders leaving office (0.088 against 0.128). On the one backtest origin never used in tuning (2015), the ensemble still beats a naive model on coups, economic collapse and autocratization; on civil war the naive rule (past conflict, size, wealth) ranked slightly better that time (AUC 0.93 against 0.90), as it did in 2000, while the ensemble led at the other six origins by 0.04–0.09. Three rounds of independent code review found about sixty real bugs, all fixed; the biggest change is a lower great-power war risk (40% to 30%). Each world now also draws which version of the onset, war-ending and coup equations is right, which widens the tails by a point or two. Since October 2026 every published version is frozen (code and forecast hashed) and scored year by year as the data arrive, next to the monthly model, the prediction markets and the AI analyst panel; on near-term questions with a liquid market the report shows the crowd's number, which beat the model on 63 resolved questions.
- **Benchmarked against the field.** Run under the rules of the 2023/24 VIEWS Prediction Challenge, the monthly fatality layer first ranked 3rd of 20 at country level over 2018–2023, with the best interval and ignorance scores, but only 10th in the live July 2024 – June 2025 window, where it under-forecast the largest wars (Ukraine, Gaza). At the 55 km grid level it is 5th of 14 over 2018–2023, within 2% of the leader, and first in the live window. Since then it predicts the change from each country's last three months, which helped on clean data (against all 48 archived ViEWS production runs it now beats ViEWS on both the chance of fighting and on deaths, including one month ahead) but not in the live window, whose most recent months come from early releases that undercounted Ukraine up to sixfold. Scaling those early releases by each country's last observed revision (damped to a quarter, chosen on the 2022–2023 revisions) cut the live error by about 6% (10th of 18). Almost all of the remaining gap was Ukraine and Gaza, wars the model expected to fade; a rule that kept very large wars going helped there but hurt 2018–2023, so it was not adopted. Since 31 October 2026 half of each forecast comes from an analog model, which finds the past stretches of any country's history most like the last twelve months and follows what came next (the idea of ShapeFinder, the challenge's leader on this score). With it the model ranks 2nd of 21 over 2018–2023, half as far behind the leader as before, keeps the best interval and ignorance scores, and moves up to 8th of 21 in both live windows. Where a prediction market or crowd covers a question, its number is better than ours, and the report shows it.
- **What else was added, and what was tested and dropped.** Each candidate was tested on history first. Kept as estimates: the working-age share (growth), leaders dying in office by age and the coups that follow an autocrat's death, science capacity beyond income, peacekeepers shortening wars, falling food-export prices raising coups, many-faction rebellions lasting longer, short-term foreign debt raising banking crises, coups spreading among countries sharing a language or colonial past, and banking crises spreading through trade (not bank lending). Kept as stated assumptions: fisheries under ocean warming and overfishing, the 2025 cuts to aid and peacekeeping, the 2025 US tariffs, and an AI dividend that fades. Dropped after testing: fish dependence and fishery disputes, fast internet and mobile spread, aid shocks, sanctions, term-limit evasion, economic complexity, coup-proofing, ethnic kin across borders, sovereign defaults and public trust. An independent review also found six design errors, now fixed, among them war losses and pandemics counted twice and great powers frozen at the 1945 five.
- **Checking the whole, not the parts.** Replayed from every year 1970–2008, the simulator gets the persistence of conflict and the way economies move together right, but shows no skill at which countries go to war at the same time, and it linked countries' wars too tightly: the world's war level spread about 1.5 times too wide. The shared "mood" shock was halved, which improved those hindcasts at 11 of 15 dev origins. Replayed from 2000–2012, it also started 17% too many wars and ended 33% too many, because each country's war risk was redrawn every year. Each simulated world now gives every country a lasting war-proneness, which cut the excess to 8% and 18% and the error in the world's war level by 12% (the median share of countries at war in 2065 rises from 14% to 15%). It also made about 1.5 times too many coup attempts from 1970–1998 and 1.65 times from 2000–2012: it took the previous decade's coup rate as its starting point, but coups have fallen in almost every decade. Starting from 70% of that rate (chosen on 1970–1998, confirmed on 2000–2012, where the country coup forecasts improved 5%) lowers the average country's chance of a coup attempt by 2030 in the simulator from 5.5% to 3.9%. Replayed from every year 1970–2012, these changes together cut the error in the world's war level by 8% and in the world coup count by 56%, with war starts, endings and coups now on target on average. Crisis data (International Crisis Behavior) cut the assumed chance that a clash between two nuclear-armed states becomes a war from 0.6 to 0.4 times the usual rate: in 39 crises between nuclear states, the only full-scale wars were fought by others. That lowers great-power war by 2065 from 69% to 59%, China–India from 52% to 39% and nuclear use from 18% to 16%. Testing the interstate war rate's calibration on history then removed a scaling to the past decade's record that did not predict the next decade: great-power war 58% to 51%, nuclear use 16% to 14%. Across 48,000 simulated worlds the assumptions explain about half of the spread in 2065 world output (mainly the AI growth dividend and arrival year) but only 1–7% of the war outcomes, which are mostly luck. From now on, a monthly job downloads UCDP's newest release, issues new 12-month forecasts and scores the old ones against persistence and against prediction markets.
- **The worst futures, and a code review (29 October 2026).** The report now shows the worst 1% of simulated worlds and traces why each is possible. Economic collapse (world GDP per person in 2065 about a third below today's) is a world where near-free AI arrives late or never, the big economies have a run of bad decades and, since the climate update, the largest climate damage estimate holds: climate is a fifth of the gap to a middle world, and without climate damage 90% of these worlds disappear. Mass war deaths (tens of millions) need a great-power war in every case, and nuclear use in a third; without allies joining wars the death tail halves. The age of civil war (over 40% of countries at war) begins with a wave of new wars and is carried by the shared world mood and by lasting war-proneness, not by any single shock. The authoritarian tail is an AI tail: early AI plus an assumed drift to autocracy. The search also found that the simulator drew small-country volatility for China and India, so world growth swung 1.7 times more than it has since 1960; scaling volatility by country size improved ten-year world GDP hindcasts by 7% (1970–1998) and 2% (2000–2012) and lowers the 2065 median from $37k to $35k. An independent code review (every finding re-checked) found no leakage of outcomes into training but about fifteen real bugs, now fixed, among them the COVID crash replayed in place of the 2021 rebound and new civil wars ending too easily (the 2065 civil-war share rises from 18% to 22%).
- **Climate risk, widened (29 October 2026).** Warming now costs the economy according to one of three published estimates, drawn per world, because history cannot tell them apart: a small level effect, Burke, Hsiang and Miguel's growth effect, or Bilal and Känzig's much larger global-temperature effect. Median world GDP per person in 2065 is $41k, $35k and $28k under each (all worlds $34k); against the same worlds without warming damage it is 14% lower in 2065 and 27% lower by 2100 on the middle emissions path (44% on the fossil-fuelled one), with a 1-in-20 chance of more than half. Harvests now replay real historical harvest years, so breadbaskets fail together as they did, and warming deepens bad years: a US cereal harvest 15% below trend, as in 1974, 1983, 1988 or 2012, comes in 6% of years now and 8% by the 2060s; two of the four big maize exporters failing in the same year rises from about 1.5% of years to 4.6%. Drylands lose cereal land as rainfall falls (an estimate from 42 dry countries since 1961). An Atlantic circulation collapse begins to bite by 2065 in about 5% of worlds and fast ice-sheet loss in 10% (assumptions). Sustained warming in farming countries fits civil war onsets historically but made forecasts worse, so it enters only as an assumption, worth about half a point of the 2065 share of countries at war. Climate now explains 9% of the spread of 2065 world output (12% in 2045, more than AI then) but almost none of the spread in wars.
- **Limits, tested (29 October 2026, evening).** The whole simulator was rerun from past starting points, 15 and 30 years ahead, with every assumption drawn at random. World growth over 30 years showed no consistent bias, and history could not narrow any of the assumed ranges: they explain only a tenth of how past worlds differed. The long runs exposed a flaw: from 1994, countries then at war (Afghanistan, Congo, Liberia, Rwanda, Angola) collapsed by 95–99% in the typical simulated world, while all of them grew. The growth equation was being pushed below any income it had seen; it is now kept within its data, which improved GDP forecasts on both test periods. Several other bugs were fixed. Population now carries the UN's own uncertainty, leaders' ages run to 2025, exposure to AI follows each country's mix of office and professional jobs, and storm exposure follows real hurricane tracks. Private debt and leaders' ethnicity were tested and rejected. Simulated countries still drift apart more than real ones did over decades, world recessions are too rare, and the democratic decline since 2000 sits at the edge of what the simulator allows. The headlines barely moved (world GDP per person in 2065 $35k; 23% of countries at war in 2065).
- **Limits, round two (30 October 2026).** Three remaining weaknesses were attacked. Democracy: free expression used to move in lockstep with the democracy index, so its erosion, which history shows comes before elections are hollowed out, could never lead in the simulation; it now has its own equation, and how personal a leader's claim to rule is enters the democracy equation. Democracy forecasts improved 1% on 1970–1998 and 5% on 2000–2012 over ten years, and up to a third over 15–30 years from 2000–2009. The model now expects less democratic progress: the world index ends near 0.40 in 2065 instead of 0.45, partly because today's personalist rule in populous countries weighs on it for decades. The 2000s democratic recession is still in the low tail of what it simulates. Countries drifting apart turned out to be a change of era: the spread is right from origins before 1990 and too wide after, because economies became calmer in the 2000s, which no history before then foretold. Weighting the replayed world shocks by GDP did not make world recessions more frequent. Banking-crisis costs, converging inflation norms and recency-weighted volatility made no difference and were dropped.
- **Who owns the AI (31 October 2026).** Like the chartered trading companies of the seventeenth century, a handful of firms own the frontier of near-free cognition, and who keeps its rents decides much of what it does to societies. Each simulated world now draws the share kept by those firms (20–90%) and how fast states take it back through tax, antitrust or nationalization. With AI from 2030, moving from the low to the high share raises the top-1% income share in 2065 from 18% to 20% and polarization in democracies by a third, and widens the lead of the frontier economies; fast clawback undoes most of it. It barely moves world democracy or war, and there is no history to test it against: it is an assumption. The labour share of income, the measurable trace of rents moving to capital, did not forecast crises, growth or democracy.
- **Other breakthroughs, in the headline (31 October 2026).** Seventeen possible breakthroughs, from fusion and cheap launch to engineered crops, longevity drugs and quantum code-breaking, are now drawn in every simulated world, with chances anchored on forecaster markets and expert surveys and arriving sooner where near-free cognition comes early. Together they raise world output per person in 2065 by about 2.5% (the headline median moves from $34.5k to $35.8k), cut oil prices by a sixth and grain prices by a tenth, and lift the Sahel and East Africa by about 8%, but leave civil war, coups and democracy where they were: technology changes economies more than the political roots of crises. Forced one at a time, the biggest levers are longevity (+7% output) and geoengineering (+6%, but 3 points more great-power war from rivalry in monsoon Asia, and a one-in-five chance per decade that spraying stops and warming rebounds, which makes grain dearer, not cheaper); on lives, fast vaccines (81 million fewer excess deaths by 2065) and engineered pathogens (187 million more). A quantum computer that breaks today's encryption costs 1.6% of world output through a wave of financial crises. Cheap space moves the chance of nuclear use by about 3 points, up or down depending on whether dominance in orbit deters or destabilises.
- **What populations do, tested (28 October 2026).** A search through other ways of modelling how populations behave (generational memory of wars and hyperinflations, expectations running ahead of reality, brittle autocracies, too many graduates for the jobs, rhetoric in the news, great-power rivalry, life satisfaction) found relationships but no forecasting gain beyond what the model already knew. Four changes passed every test and are in: how personal a regime is and who props it up (the military, foreign governments, an ethnic group), the rest of the regime's coalition for coup and autocratization forecasts only (above all a military in opposition, which comes before coups: simulated coup forecasts improved 3–4% on both test periods), the state's capacity to tax in the growth equation, and the erosion of free speech and neopatrimonial rule in the democracy equation (free speech goes before elections do). Capable states grow faster, and today's world, led by China and India, has far more of that capacity than the 1955–2024 average, so median world GDP per person in 2065 rises from $34k to $37k; the effect was confirmed over ten-year hindcasts, and beyond that it is an extrapolation. The other headlines barely moved (great-power war 47% to 48%, the 2065 civil-war share 18%).

## The longer view

The model now starts from the world of 2025. Almost one country in five has an active internal armed conflict; the post-1946 peak was 23% in 1990. The central path rises to about 21% by the mid-2030s and is about 22% in 2065. The uncertainty is the real message. By 2065 the 90% band runs from 10% to 39% of countries. In about two thirds of the simulated worlds (64%) the conflict share breaks its 1990 record at least once.

The engine was also scored on a year it had never seen. Trained only on data to 2024, it was checked against what happened in 2025. A simple monthly version beat ViEWS, the leading academic conflict-forecasting system, on its own target, using only data available in January 2025 (Brier 0.027 against 0.030). Forecasts for October 2026 to September 2027 are now on the record and will be scored as the data arrive. The annual model's clearest miss in 2025 was the United States, whose democracy index fell from 0.84 to 0.74 in one year. Starting from 2025, the model now gives the US a 28% chance of another fall of that size by 2030, up from 12%.

## Where the next crises are most likely (2026–2030)

- **New civil wars.** Chad (66%), Libya (58%), Uganda (55%), Russia (42%), Bangladesh (40%), Lebanon (39%), Côte d'Ivoire (37%), Tajikistan and Ukraine (34%; Ukraine's war with Russia is between states, so an internal war would be new), Guinea (33%). The common drivers are a recent history of conflict, neighbours at war, large and young populations, repression and polarization. Thirty-two countries were at war in 2025 and are shown separately. Myanmar, Pakistan, Nigeria, Syria and India are the most likely still to be fighting in 2030 (93–98%). In backtests, countries given 40–50% saw a new civil war about half the time.
- **Coups.** Niger (38%) and Guinea (37%), then Madagascar (36%) and Guinea-Bissau (33%), both of which had coups in 2025, Chad (33%), Mali (30%) and Burkina Faso (27%). The strongest signals are recent coups at home and next door, military-led regimes, delayed elections and, since October 2026, a military that V-Dem codes as part of the regime's opposition. In backtests the coup model ran somewhat high.
- **Economic collapse (GDP per person falling 10% or more).** Congo and Palestine (38%), Equatorial Guinea (36%), Sudan (31%), and Lebanon, Eritrea, South Sudan, Venezuela, Chad and Angola (25–29%): oil states and war economies.
- **Autocratization.** Peru (64%), Georgia (51%), Bolivia (50%), Ecuador (49%), Madagascar (47%), Tunisia, Mexico and Moldova (46%), Indonesia (43%) and Romania (40%). The leading signals are weakening courts and legislatures, polarization, mobilization against democracy and a governing party that rejects political pluralism. The United States is at 25%.

## Forty years of climate

Under the middle-of-the-road scenario (SSP2-4.5), where people live warms by another 1.2 °C on average by 2065 relative to 2015–2024. The figure is 0.8 °C with strong mitigation and 2.2 °C on the fossil-fuelled path. High latitudes warm most: Finland, Russia and Sweden gain 1.7–2.1 °C even in the middle scenario.

The economic consequences depend on which damage model you believe, and the data cannot settle it: the world has warmed too little for history to tell the estimates apart. So each simulated world draws one of three, with equal weight. A level effect (the same temperature relationship applied to the level of output rather than its growth, which fits the data as well) costs about 2–3% of world output by 2100. Burke, Hsiang and Miguel's growth effect costs 14% by 2065 and 31% by 2100 in the middle scenario, concentrated where it is already hottest: Niger, Chad, Mali, Mauritania and Burkina Faso lose 40–44% of their 2065 income, while cold, rich countries such as Russia and Canada gain. Bilal and Känzig's global-temperature estimate, which counts the extreme events that come with global warming, costs 28% by 2065 and 39% by 2100, everywhere alike. Taken together, the middle scenario costs a median 14% of world GDP per person by 2065 (90% band 0–37%) and 27% by 2100 (0–52%); the fossil-fuelled path costs 21% and 44%, and strong mitigation 10% and 14%. Each world also draws how strongly the climate responds, now with a heavier tail than the climate-model spread alone.

Harvests are where a single bad year shows. Until now the simulator moved yields with each country's yearly heat and rain, which explained only a seventh of real harvest swings: US cereal yields varied by about 1% a year in the model against 7.6% in reality, so a 2012-style Midwest drought could not happen. Each simulated year now replays the harvests of the same historical year as its economic shocks, in every country at once, so breadbaskets fail together as they did, and warming deepens the bad years by up to 30% per degree (crop models find larger losses than national data show; the range runs from none to that). A US harvest 15% below trend happened in 6% of years since 1961; the model gives 6% in the next decade and 8% by the 2060s, and a fall of 25% or more, worse than any year on record, in 27% of worlds at least once. Two of the four big maize exporters failing in the same year rises from 1.4% to 4.6% of years. Harvests move world grain prices, calories, import needs and emigration; their cost to output is already inside the damage estimates.

Dry countries lose farmland as they dry. In the 42 countries with under 600 mm of rain a year, a 10% fall in decade-average rainfall went with about 7% less cereal area since 1961 (the elasticity is 0.71, s.e. 0.29); wetter countries show nothing, and each country now follows its own rainfall projection. Two tipping points enter as assumptions: an Atlantic circulation collapse whose effects begin before 2065 in about 5% of worlds, drying the Sahel and West Africa by 10–30% and cutting north-west Europe's output and harvests, and fast ice-sheet loss that doubles or triples sea-level rise in 10% of worlds. People leave as harvests fail: about 19 million climate emigrants abroad by 2065 in the median world, 38 million in the worst 5% (an assumption scaled to the World Bank's Groundswell projections).

Does climate cause war? Weather shocks (unusual heat, rainfall or drought in a given year) did not improve the conflict, coup or collapse forecasts, nor did drought where farmers are politically excluded. Sustained warming is different: a decade of warming in countries that live from farming, or are poor, went with more new civil wars since 1960 (p about 0.02 after allowing for the common trend), but adding it made the forecasts on held-out years worse, so the link may be an artefact of the 1990s and 2010s. The simulator carries it as an assumption from no effect to about +35% per degree in a typical farming country; it moves the 2065 share of countries at war by about half a point, and the conflict fans barely differ between emissions paths.

## The United States

The state model says less than the global one, for an honest reason: all states share one business cycle, so 95 years of data hold only a handful of independent national crises.

- **Unemployment surge** (useful skill, AUC 0.77). With unemployment near record lows, the model expects a rise of 2.5 points or more somewhere in the next five years in most states: about 95% for Florida, Arizona, North Carolina, California and Maine, and about 21% for Wyoming and North Dakota. It rests on a simple regularity: each of the three recent troughs in unemployment (2000, 2007, 2019) was followed by a recession within five years.
- **Public mass shootings** (useful skill, AUC 0.74), mainly a function of population: Texas, California, Florida, Arizona and New York top the list.
- **Income shocks and housing busts** have weak skill and low forecast values; **overdose surges** proved unpredictable from state conditions (they followed national drug-supply waves) and are not forecast.

A Turchin-style structural stress index (unemployment, lost income momentum, overdoses, mass shootings, house prices relative to income, and electoral polarization) peaked in 2009, rose again in 2020, and in 2024 sits above its 1999–2024 average.

## Wars between states

The engine models about 2,120 politically relevant pairs of countries: neighbours, rivals, pairs within 1,000 km, any pair involving a major power, and pairs that have fought (from their first fight on). It learns from 1850–2025 using capabilities, power shifts, alliances, rivalries, territorial claims, nuclear status, regime mix and dispute history. In backtests it ranks the pairs that will fight within five years with an AUC of 0.96, against 0.86 for a naive rule (past fighting plus rivalry). With models fitted on data to 2024 only, it ranked the pairs that actually fought in 2025 with an AUC of 0.99, but gave the Thailand–Cambodia border war only 0.1%: sudden escalations between quiet neighbours remain its blind spot.

It reads the Second World War well. Trained only on data up to 1938, it ranks Germany–France 1st, Germany–USSR 2nd and China–Japan 3rd of 665 pairs. It reads the First World War only halfway: Germany–Russia 9th, but Germany–Britain 186th, because that war spread through alliance commitments. The simulator therefore lets allies join wars (the five permanent Security Council members anywhere, others only near the enemy) and runs a 1914-style stress test. Pairs of democracies are kept almost at peace, as they have been since 1946.

The simulated war rate starts from a violent 2025 and 2026 and settles back toward the historical level by the 2050s. Fighting already recorded for 2026 (UCDP candidate events to September: Iran against Israel and the United States, Iraq against both, Russia–Ukraine, Afghanistan–Pakistan, US–Venezuela) is treated as known. The model had given a US–Iran war 0.1% for 2026–30; even a broader model of any use of force, including strikes without deaths, gave it only 4%, because the rivalry data stop in 2010 and never coded that rivalry. The war rate is the model's own, fitted on 1946–2024. Until October 2026 it was scaled up to match the past decade's record (by 1.7 after 2015–2024), drifting back over about ten years; tested at nine origins from 1975 to 2014, the past decade's ratio did not predict the next decade, and the model as fitted was closer at eight of them, so the scaling was dropped (great-power war by 2065 58% to 51%). Run through the whole simulator from 1995 and 2004, the unscaled model comes closer from 1995 (1.21 active conflicts a year against 1.15 recorded; 1.34 with the old scaling) but further off from 2004 (0.96 against 1.4; 1.09 before), and still ranks the pairs that fought well (AUC 0.93 and 0.91). Over eight such origins from 1980 to 2014 the two versions tie. Both miss the rise of wars between states in the 2010s: from 2010 and 2014 they expect 0.7–0.9 active conflicts a year against 1.5 and 2.3 recorded, so if that rise persists these interstate risks are too low. The gap is mostly how long wars last, not how many start (from 2014 the simulator expected about 5 pairs to fight against 9, but half the conflict-years): a war still active in its third year continued 92% of the time in 2010–2024 against 59% before 1990. The duration table now weights recent decades more (half-life 25 years), which predicted continuation better on 2000–2014 origins (log-loss down 4%) and raises today's rates from 0.37, 0.48 and 0.71 to 0.43, 0.62 and 0.78 by run length; from 2010 and 2014 it could not have seen the change, which came mostly after 2018. Recurring low-level pairs (India–Pakistan, Iran–Israel) and outside powers' strike campaigns (Yemen) remain the part no tested rule anticipates. Hostility measured in news events between each pair now feeds the one-year model, fading toward the pair's own average within a few years. For 2026–2030, China–India stands out among pairs not yet fighting (37% for a 25-death conflict, 74% for any use of force). China–Taiwan is 2% for a conflict but 26% for any use of force; by 2065 they fight in 21% of worlds (a war in 9%) and China and the US in 21% (a war in 11%, one killing more than a million in 2%). A direct Russia–NATO clash happens by 2065 in 34% of worlds. A war between two great powers with at least 1,000 battle deaths a year happens by 2065 in 48% of headline worlds counting India (25% among the traditional majors); with 1914-style alliances, in 62% (42%). How big wars get is now drawn from history (Correlates of War 1816–2007, UCDP since): wars between two major powers have killed a median of about 55,000 in battle a year (the world wars far more), all others about 4,000, and the size of the belligerents adds little once that is known. Between two nuclear-armed states, new conflicts have rarely reached war level (only Kargil, 1 of 5 since 1946, against 47% of other new conflicts), and in the International Crisis Behavior data 4 of 39 crises between two nuclear-armed states reached full-scale war, all of it fought by others, against 40% of crises between non-nuclear states. So the chance that a nuclear pair's clash becomes a war is 0.4 times the usual one (range 0.1–0.7). Simulated from 1995 and 2004, total interstate battle deaths came out at a median of 96,000 and 63,000, against 110,000 and 246,000 actually counted (mostly Russia–Ukraine); both inside the 90% band. These are lower than in earlier versions: the review found that the simulator forgot each pair's real conflict history after its first year and trained the onset model partly on wars already under way.

Wars are costly in the simulation, as they were in history. Since 1951, a country where a war-level conflict was fought lost about 9% of output that year and recovered half two years later. The crisis explorer shows single cases. A China–US war over Taiwan in 2027 cuts world output by about 7.5% by 2030 and makes a great-power war by 2035 near-certain; a China–Taiwan war alone costs about 5%, mostly through the chip chokepoint (which, the review found, had been switched off outside AI scenarios). A Korean war in 2028 costs about 5%. A Gulf war with the Strait of Hormuz closed nearly doubles the oil price for a year (an assumption) and cuts world output by about 0.7% by 2030 and 1.3% by 2035; the cost falls on importers and the belligerents.

## Water and glaciers

Shared rivers do not predict wars once the model knows who is a neighbour, a rival or has fought before. Neighbours sharing a river fight 2.5 times as often in the raw data, but in backtests the water features (Oregon State's 313 international basins, 50 years of recorded water disputes, scarcity) made forecasts slightly worse. Water disputes ride on existing rivalries. Drought did not predict civil war either, even where people farm and groups are excluded from power. So water enters as an assumption whose range includes zero. Live disputes (the Indus Waters Treaty, which India has held in abeyance since April 2025; China's Medog dam on the Brahmaputra; Ethiopia's dam on the Nile; the Helmand and the Amu Darya; the Tigris and Euphrates) add up to 0.5 to the odds of fighting, and a basin partner losing scarce water adds more. With water effects off, at their sampled values and at the top of their range, India and Pakistan fight a war by 2065 in 35%, 41% and 47% of worlds, and China and India in 21%, 26% and 30%; Egypt–Ethiopia stays near 7%.

The glaciers matter less before 2065 than their reputation suggests. Using the peak-water years of three glacier models (Wimberly et al. 2025), the Indus, Tarim and Aral Sea basins are still before their peak: melt keeps rising until the 2050s, and with the CMIP6 rainfall projections Pakistan's water supply ends about 13% higher in 2065 in the median world, Uzbekistan's and Tajikistan's about 7%. The Ganges–Brahmaputra, Mekong and Salween are past their peak, but glacier melt is a small share of their flow. The squeeze comes after 2065 and in dry seasons, which the yearly model does not resolve. Egypt's water now comes from rain in Ethiopia, Uganda and South Sudan; Iraq's falls about 7% by 2065.

## Nuclear weapons, catastrophes and population

An independent review asked what the model still ignored. Several items turned out to be defects: violence by militias and against civilians was missing for every country at the 2025 origin and faded to nothing in simulation; states' territorial control and tax capacity never changed; and the governing party's hostility to pluralism, one of the strongest signals of autocratization, never changed at elections. All three now move, reverting to each country's own history. Others were tested on history and rejected: public debt does not predict financial crises here once growth and inflation are known; mineral wealth does not predict civil war; and alliances do not weaken when allies' regimes diverge (they joined wars more often, as the US did for autocratic South Korea and South Vietnam). Civil wars with outside states fighting in them end less often (−0.42 log-odds a year, UCDP), and harvests now respond to hot extremes and rainfall, with weather shocks shared across regions as observed.

The rest are assumptions with wide ranges. A nuclear weapon is used in a war by 2065 in about 14% of worlds, almost always a single use killing a median of about 130,000; a large exchange between great powers happens in about 0.2%. A new nuclear-armed state appears in 71% of worlds (Iran 53%, Saudi Arabia 22%, South Korea 14%), close to the historical pace of two or three per 40 years. A state racing for a weapon is a target for preventive war, as Iran was in 2025. A Carrington-class solar storm hits in 35% of worlds and a large volcanic eruption in 30%; each costs a year or two of output or harvests. Wars and pandemics kill a median of 17 million people beyond the UN projection (90% band 1 million to 190 million, the top set by engineered pandemics and nuclear war); world population ends within about 1% of the UN path. Each world also draws its own fertility and migration path from the UN's probabilistic projections, so country populations carry the UN's own uncertainty (world population in 2065: about ±4% at 90%). Wars now cost their trading partners more than distant countries, non-Western states fighting the West lose output to sanctions, mineral producers gain from the energy transition, and China at war with the West slows the world's clean energy and robots.

## Energy

The oil price now responds to the world the simulator creates, and 2026 is observed: crude averaged $87 a barrel in January–September against $67 in 2025, after the largest oil-supply shock in the Baumeister–Hamilton series. The price jumps about 18% when a major exporter goes to war (the same for any exporter, a known limitation), and drifts down as wind and solar spread. Wind and solar supplied 6.4% of the world's primary energy in 2024. On the S-curve fitted to the last twenty years they reach about a quarter by 2035 and 50–65% by 2065, though the ceiling is uncertain. The median real oil price ends about a third below its 2024 level. The petrostates pay for this: by 2065 Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and Norway are 13–16% poorer than on a path without the transition, and Russia about 7% poorer.

## Near-free cognition and the AI race

The scenario assumes intellectual work becomes nearly free. In the headline forecast the arrival year is uncertain (5% of worlds by 2027, 18% by 2030, 45% by 2040, 73% by 2065); the figures below fix it at 2030 to show the mechanism. Robots and autonomous weapons follow their own correlated timeline (see below). The layer was rebuilt after an independent review found real bugs. Each simulated world draws its own version of the AI future: when it arrives, how fast it spreads, how far open models trail the frontier, how much export controls slow China and Russia, and a dozen other channels within stated ranges.

- **The race.** Frontier hubs (the United States first, then China, and behind them Britain, Korea, Japan, Taiwan, Europe's leaders, Israel, Singapore, India and the Gulf) adopt first and keep extra rents. Everyone else adopts through open models a few years later. With arrival in 2030, world GDP per person in 2065 is about 2.3 times the no-AI baseline. But the United States gains 3.5 times, Korea 3.4 and Taiwan 3.1, China 2.8, Japan 2.5, Germany 2.6 and Russia 2.1, while India, Bangladesh, Vietnam, the Philippines, Nigeria and Ethiopia gain 2–35%. Robots spread more slowly than software. They add growth in the physical economy, mostly where capital and energy are plentiful, and pull manufacturing back to rich countries. The ladder that lifted East Asia is pulled up behind it. This divergence is the scenario's largest effect, and the growth dividend alone explains about 36% of the spread in 2065 world output across worlds with arrival in 2030. In the headline worlds, where the arrival year is uncertain, the timing explains 43% and the dividend 9%.
- **The chip chokepoint.** Frontier progress slows while Taiwan or South Korea is at war, and the world loses output in proportion to how much of its economy runs on cognition. That gives a Taiwan war a global cost far beyond Taiwan's share of world GDP.
- **War.** Rivals become likelier to fight when one pulls ahead in AI, as in the dreadnought race before 1914, and nuclear rivals lose some confidence in their second strike during the transition. A great-power war by 2065 rises from 43% without AI to 49% with arrival in 2030 (23% to 26% among the traditional majors).
- **Robots and autonomous weapons.** General-purpose robots arrive on their own timeline, correlated with AI (Metaculus household-robot and AI Impacts house-wiring forecasts), and come at most 3–8 years after AI. They spread over 8–20 years and add up to 5 points of growth in the physical economy (first-generation industrial robots alone added about 0.4 points a year to labour productivity). Reshoring hurts middle-income exporters when the rich economies automate. Robots raise headline world output in 2065 by about 14% ($52k to $59k) against the old setup, where robots only trailed software. Military autonomy follows the first of the two. Each world draws whether it makes wars likelier or rarer, how much longer wars last, whether rebels or states gain more in civil wars, and how far long-range strikes reach a distant power's homeland. Together these raise active interstate conflicts in 2045 from about 2.1 to 2.5 and great-power war by 2065 by about 2 points. These are assumptions with ranges that include zero; no data constrain them yet.
- **Democracy.** Surveillance makes autocracies more durable. Credentialed work loses value fastest where many graduates compete, and synthetic persuasion polarizes democracies. The world democracy index ends at 0.37 rather than 0.42; how much autocracies use surveillance is the largest single driver of that spread.
- **Money and risk.** In every scenario, including without an AI breakthrough, the 2026–2030 AI investment boom has a 20–60% chance of ending in a bust like the railway mania of 1847 or the dot-com crash of 2001, costing frontier economies 1–4%. Algorithmic finance adds financial crises, which now cost output as they did historically. Natural pandemics become rarer and milder, but engineered ones add a heavy tail: about one world in four sees one by 2065, costing 5–25% of world output in that year.
- **History as a guide.** The adoption lags come from CHAT's record of 104 technologies: a new technology now reaches a typical country in about ten years, with twelve years between early and late adopters. Electrification and computers took decades to show up in productivity. The fast scenarios here assume AI will not.

These are consequences of stated assumptions, run through relationships learned from history, not forecasts the past can check.

## Food, land and people

The simulator now keeps a food balance for every country. Harvests are yields times cereal area. Yields grow at about the world average of 1% a year, because a country's own recent trend says almost nothing about its next fifteen years. The area grows at its recent pace until it reaches the country's arable land. Population follows the UN projections, and cereal use per person rises with income (about 0.14% for each 1% of income in poor countries, less in rich ones). Where demand grows faster than harvests, imports fill the gap: Niger goes from 23% to 64% import dependency by 2065, Chad and Mali from about 10% to 56%, Ethiopia from 7% to 34%, and Egypt, already farming all its land, from 41% to 56%. A world grain price moves with oil (about a sixth of each oil move passes through), rises after poor world harvests, replays the shocks of history and is pulled back toward its post-1975 average; its median ends near today's level (87, 2025 = 100), with a 90% band of 53 to 151.

What the data do not support is the familiar story that food-price spikes cause civil wars or coups. World price spikes, weighted by each country's import dependency, added nothing to the forecasts of civil war, coups, autocratization or collapse, and national calorie supplies barely move with world prices. The link in the literature runs to riots and protests, which the model does not forecast. So food matters here through slow pressure on calories and yields, not as a trigger.

## Refugees

Refugees now flee simulated wars and settle where that country's refugees have gone in the past decade, which moves population between countries. Each country's refugee level moves toward one set by its own history and whether it is at war, estimated in one step on 1975–2025. Run from 1995, it lands on the 2015 world total (15 million simulated, 16 million recorded); run from 2005, it reaches 14 million against 31 million in 2025, missing the Ukrainian exodus and refugees from economic collapse (Venezuela). From today's 30 million, the median stays near 30 million into the 2030s and rises to about 36 million by 2065 (90% band 21–67 million), as civil wars that reach war level and wars between states displace more people. Immigration itself did not predict polarization or democratic decline in receiving countries in this data, so the simulator does not link the two.

## What an analyst panel adds

Two independent AI analysts reviewed 71 of the five-year forecasts twice, once for 2025–2029 and again on 2026-10-24 for 2026–2030, with everything they know. In the first round they knew that the India–Pakistan, Afghanistan–Pakistan and Thailand–Cambodia clashes had already happened, while the model had given the last one 0.8%. In the second round the gap on the United States has narrowed: the model now says 28% for another sharp democratic decline by 2030, against the analysts' 40% and 31%. They saw a US–Iran clash as near-certain (90–99%) where the model saw 0.1%, and they were right: UCDP's preliminary data record a US–Iran war in 2026. The model now treats recorded 2026 fighting as fact and adds a forecast of any use of force, but that rivalry was invisible to its data. They remain much more sceptical than the model about new civil wars in small states and about wars between China and India. Their judgments are shown beside the model, not blended in, and logged so they can be scored as the window closes.

## Does history move in cycles?

Psychohistory assumes that societies follow recurring patterns. Two long records were used to test this.

The first is the United States since 1790. Political violence in the following decade tracks worker immiseration (pay relative to GDP per person) and congressional polarization. Polarization in 2024 is the highest on record, above 1858. But no index of these forecasts the next decade's violence better than simply assuming it stays the same, and there is no fifty-year cycle in the data.

The second is Seshat's 169 state crises over four thousand years. Crises within a region come a median 220 years apart but no more regularly than random dates would. The risk rises only slightly as a state ages, and faster population growth is followed by more crises (58% against 42%).

The ingredients Turchin points to are real risk factors. The clockwork is not there. The engine uses the ingredients and ignores the clock.

## What the model cannot see

These are statistics about large populations, in Seldon's sense. The model cannot know a single decisive leader or a secret decision. Its data end in 2025, except for wars and oil prices already recorded in 2026. Wars, pandemics, energy and AI enter as mechanisms, learned where history allows and stated as assumptions where it does not. Space is not simulated: nothing in the data ties satellite internet or military space to the crises forecast here. Groundwater, seasonal water timing, and the escalation logic of nuclear crises are beyond its reach; the rates it uses for them are stated assumptions. Where the model, the simulator and the analyst panel disagree, the gap is a prompt to look closer, not an answer.
