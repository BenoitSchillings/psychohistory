Psychohistory model

Seldon Report
2026–2065

A statistical forecast of civil conflict, coups, economic collapse and democratic decline for every country, built from 80 years of history and 10,000 simulated futures. It speaks in probabilities about large populations, never in certainties about single events.

Validation

How much to trust this

Every number below is out-of-sample: models were trained only on what was known before each test period.

Backtest: ranking skill (AUC)

Rolling origins 1980–2015, forecasting the next five years each time. 0.5 = coin flip, 1 = perfect. Highlighted = best per row. The naive model uses only wealth, size and conflict history.

Origins used for tuning vs untouched origins

Features, hyper-parameters and ensemble weights were chosen on the 1975–2005 origins, which the table above also scores; their five-year outcomes reach 2014. The 2015 origin (outcomes 2016–2024) was never seen in any choice. Ranges are 90% intervals from resampling whole countries. On that origin the ensemble keeps a clear edge on coups, collapse, autocratization and conflict endings; on civil-war onset the naive rule ranked slightly better, as at the 2000 origin, while the ensemble led at the six others by 0.04–0.09.

Method

How the model works, and where it breaks

Data

175 countries, 1850–2025 (most series from 1950): Maddison GDP, UN population and age structure (with UN projections to 2100), V-Dem democracy, ideology, religion, coercion and coups, UCDP armed conflict, EPR ethnic exclusion, COW World Religion Project, Global Macro Database debt and crises, plus World Bank and OWID series on inequality, education and food, and ERA5 climate (temperature, rainfall, dry spells, heat extremes) with CMIP6 projections from the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal.

Predictive models

About 140 structural features grouped after structural-demographic theory (immiseration, youth bulge, elite overproduction, fiscal stress, regime type and institutions, leaders, ideology, contagion between neighbours). Six model families are trained: tuned gradient-boosted trees (on post-war data and on history since 1860), two neural network ensembles trained on the GPU, extremely randomised trees and a regularised logistic regression. Civil conflict and coups use a plain average of trees and neural network; autocratization blends short- and long-history trees with the logistic model; economic collapse relies on the quantile-input neural network with the logistic model. Each rule was chosen on 1975–2005 forecasts and confirmed on locked-away 2010–2015 forecasts.

Simulator

Each simulated world steps every country forward a year at a time. Conflict starts, ends and coup attempts are drawn from the neural hazards, aligned at the start so their average levels match the previous decade (about half of coup attempts succeed); GDP and democracy follow learned equations with conditional convergence. Shocks are resampled from history at four levels: world (a whole historical decade), region (booms and lost decades), country (decade-long growth regimes), and year. A slowly varying "world mood" lets waves of instability emerge that no structural variable foresees. Inflation and unemployment revert to each country's recent norm; rights and rule-of-law indicators move with simulated democracy. Climate follows CMIP6 projections per country and scenario, with weather noise and model spread; the Burke–Hsiang–Miguel damage function, evaluated at population-weighted temperature, lowers reported GDP for warming beyond 2024.

Two kinds of estimate

The five-year risks come straight from the ensemble. The simulator chains one-year hazards of the neural ensemble alone, so its five-year figures can differ; both are shown in the risk table.

Known limits

  • "Mules" (a new technology, a pandemic, a single leader's choices) enter only as stated scenarios (near-free cognition, the crisis explorer) or as generic shocks drawn from the past. Their odds are assumptions, not learned from data.
  • Wars between states are modelled pair by pair from a thin record (few great-power wars since 1946), so those probabilities are much less certain than the civil-conflict ones.
  • The future is built from recombined history. A world unlike 1950–2025 would fall outside the fans.
  • Debt, polarization, mobilization, political violence, inequality, poverty and military spending move in the simulation (reversion, drivers and shocks fitted on 1960 onward; 90% bands cover 85–96% of countries in hindcasts); religion and ideology are held at their last value.
  • Features, hyper-parameters and ensemble weights were chosen on backtest origins up to 2005; only the 2015 origin is untouched (see the validation table). War and climate damage lower the GDP that the conflict, coup and democracy models see, as in their training data.
  • Several sources stop before 2025 (REIGN 2021, NAVCO and MMP protests 2019; ERA5 weather now runs to 2025); their last values are carried or faded to the origin, which blurs the newest information.
  • Population follows the UN's probabilistic projections: each simulated world draws its own fertility and migration path, with the UN's spread.
  • Climate damage has no settled estimate. Each simulated world draws one of three published families (a small level effect, Burke, Hsiang & Miguel's growth effect, Bilal & Känzig's large global-temperature effect), and hindcasts cannot tell them apart because past warming is too small. Tipping points (an Atlantic circulation collapse, fast ice-sheet loss) and the climate–conflict link are stated assumptions. US state warming is pattern-scaled from national projections.
  • The US state model rests on few independent national cycles; its absolute probabilities are less reliable than its ranking of states.
  • In hindcasts the median GDP path ran about 12% too high after 20 years (−8% to +36% depending on the era), and rich economies grew too fast in the model while growth miracles such as China were under-projected. The fans covered most outcomes, but treat GDP medians as optimistic. Since the 1990s simulated countries drift apart more than real ones did, because economies became calmer than any earlier history suggested, so single-country bands are probably too wide (world growth itself showed no consistent bias in 30-year hindcasts). World recessions are too rare, and the democratic decline since 2000 sits in the low tail of the simulated range even after free expression got its own equation.
  • The GDP fan width and the world-mood volatility were chosen with the same hindcasts used to check them, so their coverage is mildly optimistic.
