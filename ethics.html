Purpose, use and ethics of named-country forecasts

6 November 2026 · Benoit Schillings

Seldon publishes probabilities of war, coups, democratic decline and economic collapse for named countries. This page says why, what the numbers are for, what they are not for, and what the project does to limit harm. It was written after a debate in which a conflict-prevention practitioner, a journalist, a government foresight client, an actuary and others argued over these questions.

Why the numbers are public

  • Hiding them would not protect anyone. Sovereign-debt desks, political-risk insurers, intelligence services and some governments already buy private country risk scores every day. If public numbers disappeared, those buyers would still have theirs, and journalists, civil society and people in the countries concerned would have none.
  • Public numbers can be checked. Every forecast is frozen and scored as outcomes arrive. Coarse public tiers ("watch", "elevated") cannot be scored, so their errors could never be shown.
  • The debate's eight participants, including the practitioner who first proposed gating the numbers, vetoed gating 7 to 1.

What the numbers are for

  • Ranking attention, not deciding action. A five-year civil-war probability helps decide where to look harder: which countries get a fresh evacuation plan, a desk report or a slot in a crisis review. It is not grounds for sanctions, intervention, divestment or force. No single number here should trigger such a decision.
  • The near term is where the record is. The monthly and one-to-five-year forecasts are scored against what happens. The 2065 simulations are conditional explorations: they show what follows from stated assumptions, many of which history cannot test.

What the project does to limit harm

  • Ranges, not bare points. Country probabilities are shown with the range across model families and as a multiple of the average country's risk. The simulator's risks for the very safest countries are too high, a documented limit, and are not reported as findings.
  • Known misses are shown next to the forecasts. Examples: the 2025 Thailand–Cambodia war, the 2026 US–Iran strikes, and the 2010s rise in wars between states.
  • No forecasts about named individuals. Seldon publishes nothing about named leaders' fate; its leader-exit model is used only to score against crowd questions in aggregate. It gives no operational detail on how a coup or war could happen. Coup risk is reported as structural vulnerability, highest in countries already under military rule.
  • No invented stories. Narrated single simulated worlds were removed from the report on 6 November 2026, because one random draw told as a story reads as a prediction.
  • Errors are corrected openly. Every change is logged with its date, and the forecast record keeps each published version unchanged.

Reflexivity

A published forecast can change what it predicts. A coup warning could, in principle, prompt pre-emptive repression, and a high collapse risk could raise borrowing costs. The project cannot measure these effects. It limits them in three ways: it publishes structural drivers rather than tactical signals, it updates the model quarterly rather than reacting to events, and it never presents a probability as a certainty. If a credible case is made that a specific published number is causing harm, the number will be reviewed and the decision logged.

Who to contact

Corrections, objections and requests to review a specific published number go to the project's author through the report site. Each is answered, and any change is logged.
